There are 13 documents in these two categories: 11 from Pope Francis and 2 from Pope Leo XIV.

The most important principle for interpreting them is that the authority of a papal document is not identical to the authority of every proposition within it. An encyclical can reaffirm an infallible teaching, present nondefinitive moral teaching, offer a prudential recommendation, and make empirical observations—all within the same document.

I've therefore marked the documents as exercises of the ordinary papal magisterium, with distinctions explained in the second table.

## 3. Institutional authority: levels of Catholic teaching and freedom to disagree

Catholic theology distinguishes three principal levels of doctrinal assent. Below them are pastoral judgments, prudential applications, empirical claims, and disciplinary regulations that require different kinds of responses.

The chart below synthesizes Lumen Gentium §25, canons 750–754, the 1998 Doctrinal Commentary on the Concluding Formula of the Professio Fidei, and Donum Veritatis (1990).

Vatican Press+3

| **Level / institutional authority** | **Definition and examples** | **Required response from Catholics** | **Freedom to disagree** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Divinely revealed, infallibly taught doctrine (de fide credenda) | Truths proposed as divinely revealed, by solemn definition or the ordinary and universal magisterium. Examples: Trinity, Incarnation, Resurrection. | Assent of divine and Catholic faith. | No doctrinal freedom to reject. Obstinate denial can constitute heresy. Questions about interpretation and development remain possible. |
| 2. Definitively held doctrine (definitive tenenda) | Teachings proposed infallibly as necessary for preserving or explaining revelation, even when not themselves declared divinely revealed. | Firm and definitive assent. | No doctrinal freedom to reject. Rejection conflicts with full communion, but is not necessarily heresy in the strict canonical sense. |
| 3. Authentic, nondefinitive magisterium | Papal or episcopal teaching on faith and morals that is authoritative but not proposed infallibly. Many teachings in encyclicals and exhortations fall here. | Religious submission of intellect and will (obsequium religiosum). | Limited, qualified room for disagreement. Serious, unresolved intellectual difficulties are recognized. Catholics are expected to study the teaching sincerely, remain open to correction, and avoid treating it as merely optional opinion. |
| 4. Prudential judgments and pastoral applications | Specific strategies applying Catholic moral principles: environmental policy, economic proposals, institutional reforms, methods of accompaniment. | Respectful and serious consideration, especially of the underlying moral principles. | Substantial freedom over particular means and judgments. Catholics may propose alternatives consistent with binding moral principles. |
| 5. Empirical, historical, or scientific assessments | Statements about climate trends, psychological development, economics, technology, or social behavior. | Evaluate evidence responsibly; consider the moral context of the teaching. | Genuine freedom to question factual accuracy. The pope's theological office does not make empirical assertions infallible. |
| 6. Ecclesiastical disciplinary rules | Liturgical, administrative, and canonical regulations established by competent Church authority. | Obedience while legitimately binding and applicable. | Freedom to advocate reform, not simply to disregard binding law. Discipline is generally changeable. |
| 7. Personal theological opinions or informal papal remarks | Speculative arguments, interviews, or personal observations not presented as authoritative doctrine. | Respectful consideration, with weight depending on context. | Broad freedom to disagree. These do not automatically constitute exercises of the magisterium. |

### Two qualifications are essential

First, level 3 is not simply an optional category. Canon 752 explicitly requires religious submission even for nondefinitive teachings. Catholic teaching does not give the faithful an unrestricted right to reject whatever they find unpersuasive.

But Donum Veritatis §§28–31 acknowledges that serious difficulties can remain after conscientious study. It distinguishes those difficulties from organized or systematic opposition to Church teaching. Its guidance is addressed specifically to theologians, although its discussion helps clarify the distinction between questioning and dissent.

Vatican

Second, the boundaries between levels 3, 4, and 5 can require careful interpretation. A pope may teach a binding moral principle and recommend a particular political or scientific response. Rejecting the proposed response is not necessarily rejecting the moral principle.

For instance, a Catholic could accept the duty to protect creation while disagreeing with a particular climate-policy recommendation.

## 4. Does an encyclical have greater authority than an apostolic exhortation?

Typically an encyclical is a more formal vehicle for substantial doctrinal or moral teaching, while an apostolic exhortation often emphasizes pastoral application, spiritual encouragement, or conclusions following a synod.

But there is no universal rule under which every encyclical is more binding than every apostolic exhortation.

Vatican II's Lumen Gentium §25 explains that a pope's teaching intention is discerned in part from the character of the document, the frequency with which a doctrine is repeated, and the manner of expression.

Vatican Press

Therefore:

- An encyclical is not automatically infallible.

- An apostolic exhortation is not automatically less binding in every respect.

- Neither document type automatically creates new dogma.

- Both can repeat teachings already considered definitive.

- Both can contain nondefinitive teaching alongside prudential and empirical judgments.

This is why assigning a single numerical authority level to an entire encyclical would be misleading.

## 5. Applying this framework to Catholic anthropology and gender

For your particular philosophical investigation, I would prioritize Amoris Laetitia §§56 and 286, while adding Dignitas Infinita (2024) as an important separate document. The latter is a declaration of the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith, not an encyclical or apostolic exhortation, and therefore is not included in the inventory above.

Here is how I would begin analyzing specific propositions:

| **Proposition** | **Authority question to investigate** |
| --- | --- |
| Human beings are created male and female. | What is established by Scripture, tradition, and definitive teaching? |
| Human sexual difference has theological significance. | Which meanings are firmly taught, and which are theological interpretations? |
| Masculinity and femininity are not rigid categories. | What does Amoris Laetitia §286 positively affirm and permit? |
| Biological sex and sociocultural gender cannot be separated. | What is the intended doctrinal force of this proposition in §56? |
| Every instance of gender-neutral pronoun usage rejects Catholic anthropology. | Has this specific inference actually been authoritatively taught? |
| A Catholic who uses she/they pronouns is outside good standing. | Is there any universal doctrinal or canonical basis for this conclusion? |

The last two propositions should not be assumed merely from the first four.

A Catholic discussion needs to distinguish what has been taught, what the teaching logically entails, and what a particular interpreter believes it entails.

For your purposes, I think that distinction is especially valuable. You can take the Church's actual doctrinal authority seriously without automatically treating every disputed philosophical inference as an equally authoritative obligation.
