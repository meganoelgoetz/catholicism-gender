## The central finding

After examining Mari Mikkola’s Feminist Perspectives on Sex and Gender alongside your Catholic sources, I think the most important conclusion is this:

Contemporary feminist philosophy and Catholic anthropology are not necessarily opposed on the relationship between biological sex and gender. They often begin with compatible observations about embodiment, culture, and human identity, but can arrive at different conclusions about what ultimately constitutes a person as male, female, a woman, or a man.

The deepest disagreement is not whether biology exists, whether culture shapes gender, or whether people should be free from restrictive gender stereotypes.

It is about what kind of reality gender is.

There are several possibilities:

- Gender is the cultural interpretation of biological sexual difference.

- Gender is a social position or relationship, shaped partly by how others interpret our bodies.

- Gender is an aspect of personal identity, which may not correspond straightforwardly to biological classification.

- Gender is something constituted through repeated social actions and relationships.

- Gender is an intrinsic dimension of our embodied human nature, with significance beyond social conventions.

These are not all mutually exclusive, but neither are they equivalent.

An important discovery in Mikkola's article is that feminist philosophers disagree substantially with one another about these alternatives. Some reject the conventional sex/gender distinction altogether. Others give biological sex a foundational role while rejecting claims that biology determines personality, social vocation, or appropriate behavior.

Likewise, the Catholic intellectual tradition contains more than one philosophical account of how sexual difference relates to personal identity.

I will distinguish three levels throughout this analysis: what Mikkola reports about feminist philosophy, what Catholic teaching actually asserts, and what I think can reasonably be synthesized from the two.

## 1. Summary of Mikkola's article

Source: Mari Mikkola, Feminist Perspectives on Sex and Gender, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Summer 2024 edition.

Mikkola's article is a survey of competing philosophical positions rather than a defense of one theory. Its arguments develop through three broad problems.

### The first problem: Does biology determine gender?

Historically, feminist philosophers developed the sex/gender distinction to resist biological determinism: the belief that biological differences between females and males determine their psychology, abilities, social roles, or proper place in society.

Simone de Beauvoir, Gayle Rubin, and others challenged the assumption that characteristics associated with femininity necessarily follow from female biology.

Their argument was not necessarily that biological sex is unreal. Rather, they questioned the inference from a biological fact to a social prescription.

Consider the difference between these propositions:

Female bodies are ordinarily organized around the biological possibility of gestation.

And:

Women are naturally more nurturing, receptive, passive, and suited to caregiving than men.

The first concerns reproductive biology. The second introduces psychological generalizations and normative expectations that require independent justification.

This distinction is the foundation of much subsequent feminist philosophy.

### The second problem: What actually constitutes gender?

The theorists disagree sharply about the answer.

| **Philosopher** | **Central account of gender** |
| --- | --- |
| Simone de Beauvoir | Womanhood as lived and socially formed, rather than merely determined by anatomy |
| Gayle Rubin | Gender as the social organization and interpretation of sexual difference |
| Nancy Chodorow | Masculine and feminine personalities developing through family relationships and early psychological formation |
| Catharine MacKinnon | Gender constituted through hierarchical relationships of sexual dominance and subordination |
| Judith Butler | Gender constituted and reproduced through repeated, socially meaningful acts |
| Sally Haslanger | Gender understood as a social class defined through sex-marked privilege and subordination |
| Charlotte Witt | Gender as a fundamental social role that organizes a person's other social roles |
| Linda Martín Alcoff | Gender as social positionality shaped by reproductive embodiment, lived experience, and cultural expectations |
| Robin Dembroff | Gender categories understood partly through their relation to dominant gender norms, including nonbinary challenges to those norms |

These theories cannot simply be collapsed into a single conception of gender.

For example, Chodorow explains gendered psychological differences through development. MacKinnon explains gender through power. Butler questions the very assumption that a stable feminine essence exists. Alcoff offers an account in which bodies matter, but their significance is mediated by social experience.

### The third problem: Can we distinguish sex and gender so neatly?

This is where Mikkola's article becomes particularly interesting for Catholic philosophy.

Some feminist philosophers eventually become dissatisfied with the original sex/gender distinction.

Toril Moi, for example, argues that treating biological sex as nature and gender as culture creates an artificial division. Human beings are embodied subjects whose experiences cannot be understood adequately by placing nature and culture into separate compartments.

Judith Butler makes a different argument: even the classification of physical bodies as male or female takes place within systems of social meanings. Butler does not simply claim that physical bodies do not exist. The argument is that our interpretation and classification of those bodies cannot be entirely detached from social practices.

Linda Alcoff then offers a particularly useful alternative: reproductive possibilities are real, but their influence on a person's social identity emerges through culturally and historically situated experience.

Mikkola also examines criticisms that theories of womanhood impose a single supposedly universal experience. Elizabeth Spelman points out that race, class, nationality, and cultural circumstances profoundly affect what it means to live as a woman.

The challenge is to explain what women share without asserting that all women share one psychological profile, life experience, or social position.

### A further development: Gender beyond the binary

Dembroff argues that conventional accounts of men and women are inadequate for explaining nonbinary identities.

Their account distinguishes between political resistance to gender norms and a person's own experience of gender categorization. These can overlap, but they are not identical.

This matters because identifying as nonbinary need not express only an individual psychological state or only a political objection to gender stereotypes. It can involve embodied experience, personal identification, and social relationships.

Mikkola closes the article without endorsing one definitive theory. The metaphysics of gender remains unsettled.

## 2. What does the Catholic intellectual tradition contribute?

The Catholic tradition starts with a somewhat different philosophical project.

Feminist philosophers frequently ask how gender categories arise, how they function, and how they affect social justice.

Catholic anthropology also asks a metaphysical and theological question: What is the human person, and what significance does sexual difference have within creation?

Four Catholic commitments are especially relevant.

### A. The human person is an embodied unity

The Aristotelian-Thomistic tradition understands the soul as the form of the body.

The[ ](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_one/section_two/chapter_one/article_1/paragraph_6_man.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)[Catechism §§362–365](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_one/section_two/chapter_one/article_1/paragraph_6_man.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com) explicitly teaches that body and soul constitute one human nature.

This rejects an account of the person in which consciousness is the real self and the body is merely an external object possessed or controlled by that self.

But an important distinction follows.

Affirming that embodiment is constitutive of human personhood does not, by itself, establish that every aspect of gender identity, personality, and social role is biologically determined.

A Thomistic account of the person can accommodate the idea that biological embodiment influences experience while also recognizing the importance of habits, education, relationships, and historical circumstances.

This creates a significant opening for dialogue with feminist theories of embodied subjectivity.

### B. Sexual difference possesses intrinsic significance

Catholic teaching goes beyond the assertion that human beings have sexually differentiated bodies.

The[ ](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_three/section_two/chapter_two/article_6/i_male_and_female_he_created_them.index.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)[Catechism §§2331–2335](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_three/section_two/chapter_two/article_6/i_male_and_female_he_created_them.index.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com) describes sexuality as affecting the whole person, including emotional life, capacities for love, procreation, and communion.

John Paul II develops a stronger account in[ ](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/apost_letters/1988/documents/hf_jp-ii_apl_15081988_mulieris-dignitatem.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)[Mulieris Dignitatem](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/apost_letters/1988/documents/hf_jp-ii_apl_15081988_mulieris-dignitatem.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com) and his[ ](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/letters/1995/documents/hf_jp-ii_let_29061995_women.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)[Letter to Women](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/letters/1995/documents/hf_jp-ii_let_29061995_women.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com).

For John Paul II, complementarity is not merely anatomical or functional. He proposes that it has psychological and ontological dimensions as well.

This is a more substantial commitment than simply affirming biological sex.

The International Theological Commission's[ ](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/cfaith/cti_documents/rc_con_cfaith_doc_20040723_communion-stewardship_en.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)[Communion and Stewardship §33](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/cfaith/cti_documents/rc_con_cfaith_doc_20040723_communion-stewardship_en.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com) similarly distinguishes changing social roles from sexual identity, which it understands as constitutive of personal identity rather than simply a cultural construction.

Here is the central philosophical difficulty.

If sexual difference establishes objectively different ways of being human, what are those differences?

Reproductive organization establishes some real bodily differences.

But does it also establish fundamentally different modes of thinking, feeling, reasoning, loving, or relating to God?

Catholic theologians sometimes answer yes, but the further argument matters. The existence of reproductive differences does not logically establish a corresponding differentiation of all psychological or spiritual characteristics.

That additional step requires philosophical or theological premises.

### C. Catholic teaching recognizes cultural variation

Pope Francis's Amoris Laetitia §286 makes an especially important qualification.

He expressly recognizes that masculinity and femininity develop through biology, temperament, culture, experience, education, and relationships.

He criticizes restrictive expectations about which activities are appropriate for men and women, and argues that such expectations can interfere with people's development.

His explicit statement is:

Masculinity and femininity are not rigid categories.

Vatican

But Francis does not conclude that biological sexual difference is irrelevant to personal identity.

He maintains that masculine and feminine existence must be understood in relation to creation.

This establishes two simultaneous propositions within contemporary Catholic teaching.

Sexual difference matters, but conventional masculine and feminine behavior does not exhaust its meaning.

What remains less precisely specified is the positive content of masculinity and femininity once culturally contingent characteristics have been distinguished from them.

### D. Human dignity precedes gender identity and performance

The Catholic tradition also supplies an important criticism of theories that reduce the person to gendered actions, social categories, or self-understanding.

In[ ](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_one/section_two/chapter_one/article_1/paragraph_6_man.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)[Catechism §357](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_one/section_two/chapter_one/article_1/paragraph_6_man.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com), the human person possesses inherent dignity as someone capable of self-knowledge, freedom, and communion.

Dignitas Infinita grounds that dignity in the person's very being, rather than in performance, social recognition, or the person's success in articulating their identity.

This creates a potential synthesis: gendered experiences and social identities can be profoundly meaningful without constituting the ultimate foundation of personhood.

## 4. The difficult question in Dignitas Infinita

The most consequential recent Catholic statement is Dignitas Infinita §§55–60.

The declaration criticizes accounts of gender that deny sexual difference or understand identity as radically independent of biological embodiment. It also describes unrestricted personal self-determination as incompatible with the creature's obligation to receive human nature as a gift.

In §59 it repeats the proposition from Amoris Laetitia that biological sex and sociocultural gender may be distinguished but not separated.

Vatican

The key interpretive question is what separation means.

There is an important distinction between these claims:

Sex and gender are distinguishable concepts.

Gender roles and expectations are not identical to reproductive biology. Catholic teaching explicitly acknowledges this.

Sex and gender are related in embodied human existence.

The social experience of gender can be influenced by the body's characteristics, possibilities, and the ways others respond to them.

Sex necessarily entails a distinct masculine or feminine psychological essence.

This is a much stronger claim. The cited statement about nonseparation does not, on its own, establish precisely which psychological characteristics would be essential.

The strongest Catholic accounts of ontological complementarity do propose something beyond the first two claims. Those accounts must be taken seriously.

But their claims should not be confused with the more modest observation that sexual embodiment is real and significant.

### Is the Catholic criticism of gender theory addressing the actual philosophical literature?

Sometimes it is, and sometimes it appears insufficiently differentiated.

Consider Butler. Butler's theory poses significant challenges to Catholic ideas about stable sexual identity and the created significance of sexual difference. There is a genuine philosophical disagreement here.

Nevertheless, Butler's account of gender performativity is not accurately summarized as the proposition that individuals can simply choose gender independently of all bodily and social conditions.

Similarly, Alcoff's theory explicitly incorporates reproductive embodiment. It would be inaccurate to characterize her position as denying biological sexual difference.

The danger of an overly general critique of gender ideology is that rejecting a particular theory can be mistaken for rejecting the entire field of gender philosophy.

The reverse mistake is also possible. Showing that some feminist philosophers acknowledge embodiment does not establish that their entire accounts of gender are compatible with Catholic doctrine.

The actual arguments must be evaluated individually.

## 5. The Catholic thinkers in your excerpts add another dimension

Your Catholic Writings on Gender Excerpts includes an illuminating disagreement between Paul J. Griffiths and Daniel Walden.

It also includes summaries advocating respect for chosen pronouns and a subsequent interpretive discussion. Those contributions should be distinguished from the magisterial excerpts: they represent theological arguments and commentary, not official doctrinal definitions.

### Griffiths: Gender as enacted relationship

Griffiths rejects the idea that we need a hidden, internally possessed gender essence to explain gendered life.

He understands gender as performed within social and relational practices.

Drawing on Wittgenstein and Trinitarian theology, he suggests that our gendered identities are constituted through the ways we relate to others.

This resembles Butler's performative account in some respects, although Griffiths develops his argument around Christian relationality and gift-giving.

Importantly, Griffiths's position does not require people to conform to stereotypical masculinity or femininity. He explicitly welcomes improvisation and the disruption of rigid gender conventions.

### Walden: Gender as lived and narrated experience

Walden emphasizes that human beings must be allowed to interpret and communicate their own experiences.

He does not maintain that self-interpretation is infallible.

Rather, understanding another person's account of themselves is necessary before making a responsible judgment about it.

The contrast is subtle but important.

For Griffiths, gendered actions constitute gender.

For Walden, gendered actions and descriptions participate in an ongoing activity of understanding and communicating one's life.

Walden's approach gives greater weight to interior experience, intention, and the person's own account of what their actions mean.

### How I would synthesize them

I think Walden and Griffiths each identify a real difficulty.

Griffiths is correct to question whether every meaningful gender identity must correspond to some separately identifiable metaphysical essence.

Walden is correct that human experience cannot be interpreted adequately from external behavior alone.

Catholic moral philosophy has conceptual resources for preserving both insights.

Human actions possess outward forms, but their meaning also involves intention, circumstances, relationships, and moral understanding.

A person's self-description is meaningful evidence about their interior life. It is neither automatically infallible nor something that other people can simply disregard.

This is also the direction suggested by the interpretive discussion included in your excerpts.

## 6. My proposed philosophical synthesis

I think the most promising synthesis is an account of gender as embodied, socially mediated, and personally interpreted existence.

This is a proposed philosophical framework, not a formula officially adopted by the Church.

FOUNDATION

Embodied human nature

Human bodies are real, sexually differentiated, and integral to personal existence. They are not merely external possessions.

SOCIAL EXPERIENCE

Culturally mediated gender

Societies interpret bodily differences, organize relationships, distribute responsibilities, and construct expectations about masculinity and femininity.

PERSONAL EXPERIENCE

Lived identity and self-understanding

Individuals encounter their embodied and social existence through their own temperament, history, relationships, desires, and reflective understanding.

MORAL AND RELATIONAL LIFE

Expression, freedom, and discernment

People communicate and negotiate gendered meanings in relationships with others, subject to moral responsibilities, human dignity, and the limits of self-knowledge.

Conceptual framework for discussion, not an official Catholic model. These dimensions interact in both directions rather than forming a strictly one-way causal sequence.

This framework draws especially on Alcoff's embodied positionality, Walden's account of personal interpretation, Griffiths's relational emphasis, and Catholic philosophical commitments concerning embodiment and personhood.

It allows us to distinguish embodied sexual difference from the social and psychological meanings attributed to that difference without making them unrelated.

It also does not require every meaningful personal identity description to designate an independent metaphysical essence.

However, it does not resolve all disagreements. A theologian committed to strongly differentiated masculine and feminine ontologies may argue that the framework underestimates the intrinsic significance of sexual difference. A philosopher committed to fully nonbinary gender ontology may argue that it still privileges reproductive classification too heavily.

Those are substantive objections requiring further argument.

I think this is the most productive question for continuing the investigation.

Consider a female person who acknowledges her biology, values her embodied life, but does not experience femininity as a distinct psychological essence.

She might regard biological sex as meaningful without regarding the category of feminine personality as an accurate description of herself.

What precisely would Catholic anthropology require her to affirm beyond her embodied reality?

A strong complementarian might respond that femininity constitutes an objective dimension of her personhood, even if she does not subjectively recognize it.

A feminist philosopher influenced by Alcoff might respond that the person's embodied situation is real but that its psychological and social significance emerges through individual experience and cultural interpretation.

A Catholic philosopher influenced by Walden might add that her account of herself deserves serious interpretive attention without treating her self-description as the ultimate authority over metaphysical truth.

This creates a more precise debate than simply asking whether someone accepts or rejects gender ideology.

It asks whether an objective distinction between male and female persons entails an additional distinction between masculine and feminine psychological or spiritual modes of existence.

Those are two different propositions.

## 8. Where I ultimately land

My provisional conclusion is that a substantial synthesis is possible, but its boundaries must be acknowledged.

The strongest point of convergence is the rejection of a simplistic account in which biological sexual differences automatically determine personality, abilities, or social vocation. Many feminist theories and contemporary Catholic teaching can agree here.

The strongest genuine disagreement concerns whether sexual difference entails enduring masculine and feminine dimensions of personal identity beyond reproductive embodiment and its social consequences. Some Catholic texts affirm this strongly; many feminist philosophers question it.

The most important unresolved question is whether those additional dimensions can be specified philosophically without relying on culturally contingent understandings of masculinity and femininity.

This does not mean Catholic teaching is entirely indeterminate. Catholic doctrine makes positive claims about created embodiment, sexual difference, and human communion, and recent magisterial documents reject certain accounts of self-determination and gender identity.

But neither does it mean that every contested philosophical explanation of masculinity and femininity possesses equal doctrinal authority.

There is a difference between receiving an authoritative teaching about the human person and accepting every metaphysical, psychological, or cultural inference someone draws from that teaching.

I would focus the next stage of this project on a close comparison of Linda Alcoff's embodied positionality, Catholic Thomistic anthropology, and John Paul II's ontological complementarity.

Those three positions provide an especially productive test of whether the Catholic intellectual tradition requires a distinct feminine essence—or whether the Catholic understanding of sexual embodiment can accommodate a broader, less psychologically prescriptive account of gender.

That question gets closer to the real philosophical disagreement than the broad opposition between Catholicism and feminism
