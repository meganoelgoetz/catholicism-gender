# Catholic Teaching Authority, Conscience, Dissent, and Our Theological Questions

**A consolidated reference from the October 2026 discussions**  
**Prepared October 21, 2026**

## Purpose and scope

This document consolidates the recent discussions about Catholic teaching authority, the permissible scope of theological disagreement, conscience, Catholic belonging, sex and gender, pronouns, complementarity, and contraception/natural family planning (NFP). It is meant to be a careful reference rather than an argument that every personal conviction is consistent with official Catholic teaching.

**Sources of this synthesis.** The personal positions and previously discussed sources are reconstructed from available summaries of our October 20–21 conversations, not from a complete verbatim transcript of every earlier exchange. The doctrinal descriptions were independently checked against official Vatican texts. Items labeled **previously discussed** are supported by the conversation summaries; items labeled **supplemental reference** were added for context and should not be mistaken for something we necessarily covered earlier. Claims about a particular doctrine's *definitive* status are not inferred merely from the title, prominence, or strong language of a document.

**Central findings.** Catholic teaching has three formally distinguished levels of doctrinal assent: revealed doctrine believed with divine and Catholic faith; other teachings definitively to be held; and non-definitive authentic magisterial teaching requiring religious submission of intellect and will. A fourth, different axis consists of ecclesiastical laws, prudential judgments, pastoral directions, and applications; theological opinions and a spouse's private interpretations have still another status. A Catholic can experience profound difficulty with teachings, and conscience must not be coerced; neither point establishes a general ecclesial right to reject authentic teaching without consequence. Equally, the fact that a spouse invokes “Catholic anthropology” does **not** turn every inference about another person's pronouns, motives, marriage, or integrity into a binding teaching of the Church.

## 1. The essential distinctions

**Revelation, interpretation, and individual inference are not interchangeable.** In Catholic theology, Scripture and apostolic Tradition constitute the deposit of faith; the Magisterium serves and authentically interprets that deposit, rather than standing above it (*Dei Verbum* 10). Catholic doctrine may be proposed at different levels of definitiveness. The *Catechism* summarizes Church teaching but is not a uniform list of dogmatic definitions: the authority of a particular proposition depends on its substance and how the Church has proposed it. A dicastery declaration, conciliar constitution, papal encyclical, canon-law norm, theological article, and private comment by clergy are **not the same kind of statement**.

**Level of teaching is not the same as document format.** An ecumenical council can teach infallibly or non-definitively; an encyclical can contain weighty authentic doctrine without being an *ex cathedra* definition; a document can repeat an older definitive teaching without creating a new definition. The 1998 *Doctrinal Commentary* and *Lumen gentium* 25 insist that the precise proposition, manner of proposal, repetition, and manifest intent matter. Canon 749 §3 cautions that no doctrine is understood as infallibly defined unless this is manifestly evident.

**Religious submission is not the same as absolute certainty.** For non-definitive teaching, canon 752 calls for religious submission of intellect and will. That is more than polite acknowledgment or external compliance, but it does not convert the teaching into an irreformable dogma. *Donum veritatis* recognizes serious difficulties with non-irreformable teachings, while sharply rejecting a generalized “right to dissent.”

## 2. Master chart: levels of authority and assent

| Category | What makes a teaching fall here | Response expected of the faithful | Scope for disagreement and correction | Key sources |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **I. Divinely revealed doctrine (*de fide credenda*)** | Proposed as revealed by a solemn definition or the ordinary and universal Magisterium. Can be taught infallibly without a new solemn definition. | **Theological faith**: believed with divine and Catholic faith. | Obstinate post-baptismal denial/doubt of a truth known to be in this category is the canonical substance of heresy; sincere questioning is not identical to obstinate denial. No recognized doctrinal freedom to reject such a truth as false. | *Professio fidei* 1998, first paragraph and Commentary §§5, 9; canons 749–751. |
| **II. Definitively proposed doctrine (*de fide tenenda*)** | Doctrine of faith or morals definitively to be held, even when not proposed as divinely revealed; needed to guard or expound revelation faithfully. | **Firm and definitive assent**: accepted and held. | No authorized option to reject as false. Rejection contradicts Catholic doctrine, but is **not automatically the same juridic category as heresy** under canon 751. | *Professio fidei* 1998, second paragraph and Commentary §§6–9; canon 750 §2. |
| **III. Authentic but non-definitive teaching** | Pope or college of bishops teaches faith/morals authentically without intending a definitive act; local bishops also teach authentically within their competence. | **Religious submission of intellect and will**, proportional to the teaching's character and authoritativeness. It is not merely outward silence. | Serious, reasoned difficulty may remain; theologians are instructed to study, seek clarification, engage responsible authorities, and remain open to correction. Not a license for casual or organized public repudiation. Such teaching may be developed or corrected. | *Lumen gentium* 25; canon 752 (and 753 for bishops); *Donum veritatis* §§23–31. |
| **IV. Ecclesiastical discipline, governance, or contingent prudential application — separate axis, not a fourth *Professio fidei* level** | Valid church law, administrative measures, pastoral instructions, or judgments combining enduring moral principles with contextual facts. | Legitimate laws and directives may bind conduct by office or jurisdiction; a prudential claim does not automatically require the assent owed to dogma. | Room for policy debate, exceptions where law provides them, appeal, and revision depends on the measure. Avoid treating a changeable rule as a divinely revealed proposition; equally, “prudential” does not mean “can be ignored.” | Canons 212, 754; *Donum veritatis* §24; relevant governing law. |
| **V. Theological proposals, expert analyses, customary assumptions, or private readings — not magisterial doctrine as such** | Statements by theologians, clerics speaking privately, educators, spouses, popular apologetics, or arguments not formally taught with binding authority. | Fair consideration on their merits, **not ecclesial assent by virtue of the speaker alone**. | Wide scope to question, reject, refine, or test against official sources, while respecting persons. | *Dei Verbum* 10; 1998 Commentary §4; distinction of magisterium from theology in *Donum veritatis* §§21–22. |

**Critical caution:** Categories IV and V are **different kinds of authority**, not official “fourth and fifth grades” in the 1998 profession. Moreover, individual assertions within one document may draw their authority from earlier, stronger teachings. **Strong language is not itself proof of infallibility; non-infallibility is not proof of optionality.**

## 3. Master chart: what does “free to dissent” actually mean?

| Situation | What Catholic sources affirm | What they do **not** thereby grant | Practical characterization |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **Asking questions, seeking reasons, admitting inability to assent immediately** | Faith seeks understanding; the faithful must form conscience. *Donum veritatis* anticipates tensions and serious difficulties. | A guaranteed right to treat any teaching as simply one opinion. | **Permissible inquiry / real difficulty**, distinct from deliberate rejection. |
| **Rejecting revealed dogma as false** | The Church teaches this demands divine and Catholic faith. | Doctrinal permission to negate a dogma while claiming the negation as a Catholic belief. | **Grave doctrinal conflict**; obstinacy and other canonical criteria matter when applying the category of heresy. |
| **Rejecting a definitively held doctrine** | Definitive assent is required although it need not be proposed as revealed. | A free option to prefer its contrary. | **Grave doctrinal conflict**, technically distinct from the definition of heresy in canon 751. |
| **Having sustained difficulty with a non-definitive teaching** | *Donum veritatis* §§24–31 allows for the possibility of well-founded difficulty and counsels careful investigation and dialogue. | That non-definitiveness makes assent optional or subjective conscience the sole authority on doctrine. | **A real but constrained space for difficulty and dialogue.** |
| **Publicly organizing or proclaiming dissent as a rival authoritative rule** | Faithful have qualified rights to communicate concerns (canon 212 §§2–3). | An unqualified Church-recognized “right to dissent”; *Donum veritatis* §36 explicitly denies that the free act of faith establishes one. | **Church teaching restricts this**, especially for official teachers; the details depend on role and conduct. |
| **Following a certain judgment of conscience in one's own action** | *Catechism* 1790 says one must obey a certain conscience; 1782 protects the dignity of moral decision; 1783–85 require formation. | That sincerity makes every moral judgment objectively correct or cancels duties to study Church teaching. | **An obligation to conscience coupled with an obligation to form it.** |
| **Refusing coercion or a false profession of belief** | *Dignitatis humanae* §§2–3, 10 and canon 748 §2 reject forcing someone to embrace faith against conscience. | A right to dictate what the Church officially teaches or to hold every Church office regardless of required profession. | **Freedom from coerced belief**, not an ecclesial veto over teaching. |
| **Respectfully offering concerns to pastors and, in appropriate conditions, other faithful** | Canon 212 §3 recognizes a right and sometimes a duty to express informed views for the Church's good, with reverence and regard for the integrity of faith/morals. | An unrestricted entitlement to represent one's position as official doctrine. | **A genuine but qualified voice within the Church.** |
| **Continuing to identify with the Catholic Church while unsettled about teaching** | Baptism is enduring; canons 204–205 distinguish incorporation and the bonds of full communion. | An automatic assurance that every disputed position is compatible with full ecclesial communion; nor does every private doubt trigger excommunication. | **Identity, faith, communion, sacramental discipline, and canonical penalties are different questions.** |

### A precise reconciliation of conscience and assent

These claims can be true simultaneously: **(1)** the Church demands substantial assent to authentic teaching, including non-definitive teaching; **(2)** one cannot manufacture belief by being pressured; **(3)** a person must follow a certain judgment of conscience in concrete action, even while recognizing that conscience can be mistaken; and **(4)** sincere disagreement does not authorize rewriting the Church's doctrinal position. The hard part of the discussion was not choosing one sentence and discarding the others, but acknowledging their tension. “Respectful dissent” may describe a person's manner of disagreement, but it is **not** a formal fourth category of authorized doctrinal assent.

*Donum veritatis* is particularly important here because its specific treatment of a **theologian's** difficulty must not be inaccurately expanded into an unrestricted entitlement for every Catholic, nor interpreted as a ban on laypeople asking questions or voicing concerns. Canon 212 supplies the separate framework for the faithful's expressed views. The document urges private and competent engagement rather than rash public pressure campaigns; it also acknowledges that prudential interventions may include contingent features or deficiencies.

### How to read this chart

“Official,” “important,” “approved,” and “infallible” are **not synonyms**. A decree's authority must be interpreted in relation to its content, author, stated intention, and continuity with other teaching. In particular: it would be incorrect to assert from this inventory alone that the specific Church condemnation of contraception has been proven to meet the criteria for an infallible definition; it would be **equally incorrect** to infer that official Catholic teaching leaves contraception morally optional. Conversely, a local cleric's claim that using a particular pronoun *necessarily* entails an entire philosophical anthropology is not made magisterial merely because it draws upon official texts.

## 5. The theological issues we actually explored

### A. Sexed embodiment, gender roles, and “Catholic anthropology”

**Official Catholic position.** The *Catechism* §§2331–33 treats sexuality as affecting the whole embodied person and describes male/female difference and complementarity in relation to marriage and family. *Dignitas infinita* §59 states that biological sex and gender's socio-cultural role may be **distinguished, not separated**; it rejects accounts that efface sexual difference. The 2000 Pontifical Council text likewise separates analytically psychobiological sex identity and psychosocial gender identity while arguing for integration.

**The user's articulated view.** Being biologically female is acknowledged. The user has sometimes used **she/they** and described her gender experience as nuanced, without asking her husband to switch to they/them. She does not regard that limited pronoun choice as a repudiation of her body, her sex, or her marriage. She is skeptical that a descriptive sex difference must become a prescribed role or an exhaustive ideal of personhood.

**The disagreement.** Justin regarded the pronoun disclosure as raising “fundamentally different anthropologies.” There is a genuine theological question here: official Catholic sources do attach meaning to bodily sexual difference, so it would be misleading to claim that the Church has no position on embodied sexual identity. But a **further inference** is required to claim that a woman using she/they thereby denies sex, affirms a specific metaphysical theory, repudiates marriage, or acted deceptively. None of the reviewed authoritative sources explicitly establishes **that particular pronoun-to-doctrine inference**. A lack of such an explicit rule does not mean the Church positively approves every meaning someone might give those pronouns.

**Working distinction.** “This choice raises questions for my theological anthropology” is different from “this choice proves you have rejected Catholic anthropology” or “you intentionally concealed a new identity.” The first can be a sincere concern; the latter requires evidence about the person's actual beliefs and intentions.

### B. Complementarity: biological reality versus normative ideal

**Official position.** Catholic documents present complementarity as more than a population-level biological observation: they make theological and moral claims about the relationship of men and women, marriage, and human flourishing. *Catechism* §§2333 and 372 and *Gaudium et spes* §§49–50 are key texts. These texts also affirm equal human dignity and, in the case of marriage, mutual rather than unilateral self-giving.

**The user's position.** She accepts that male/female complementarity has a biological or statistical dimension but rejects turning that into a blanket moral ideal that every person must fulfill in a particular gendered way. She has articulated that sex/gender integration might be presented as an ideal yet not fit every life, and that enforcing conformity can itself cause harm.

**Point of genuine doctrinal friction.** This objection does reach beyond mere pronouns. The Church's texts explicitly attach moral significance to sexual difference; a position rejecting **any** normative moral significance of complementarity is not simply the official Catholic position under a different vocabulary. However, a challenge to **particular gender roles, stereotypes, or a spouse's extrapolations** from complementarity is not necessarily a denial of the Church's teaching on difference. The exact proposition disputed matters.

### C. Contraception, periodic continence, and disproportionate burdens

**Official position.** *Humanae vitae* §§14–16 and *Catechism* §2370 distinguish contraceptive acts from periodic abstinence, judging the former impermissible and the latter morally permissible when practiced responsibly. This is a real official teaching and is not transformed into an open question by pointing out that its exact theological note is debated. *Gaudium et spes* §50 addresses common counsel by spouses, considering the needs of spouses and children, and formation of conscience in relation to Church teaching.

**The user's experience and argument.** The couple has used NFP rather than contracepting. The user reports that avoiding potentially fertile periods limited marital intimacy and harmed the sexual relationship, with the burdens of unintended pregnancy and fertility management falling unevenly on her. Pregnancy decisions also intersect with bodily risks, finances, and capacity to care for family. She questions a theological ideal that can leave these asymmetries inadequately addressed.

**Analytical distinction.** Reporting that NFP has been harmful **is not itself the same claim** as advocating contraception. But a conclusion that contraception is morally acceptable in cases the Church prohibits it would conflict with current official Catholic moral teaching. Conversely, *Humanae vitae*'s moral rule does **not** decide whether a husband is giving adequate attention to his wife's health, burdens, consent, agency, or need for intimacy. Those are serious, distinct moral questions. A moral norm should not function as a conversation-ending answer to the unequal distribution of costs.

### D. Conscience, sincere non-assent, and coerced profession

The question raised was not merely “Can I do whatever I prefer?” It was whether a person who takes Catholicism seriously can admit a deep inability to affirm certain propositions without pretending they have changed their mind. A repeated concern was whether verbally submitting under relational or religious pressure constitutes meaningful assent.

The Church teaches both that the act of faith must be free (*Dignitatis humanae* §10; canon 748 §2) and that a Catholic owes different kinds of assent to official teaching. *Catechism* §1790 directs persons to obey their **certain** conscience, while §§1783–85 and 1791–93 require the conscience to be formed and concede it can err. Taken together, these texts mean that an honest statement such as “I understand what the Church teaches, and I am still unable to make that conviction my own” is not interchangeable with either a knowing lie of assent or a declaration that the Church must change its teaching. It can describe a difficult, theologically unresolved state.

The distinction between **interior unresolved difficulty**, **personal conduct**, **public teaching**, and **formal rejection of a defined article of faith** must be maintained. Those acts do not automatically have the same doctrinal or canonical significance. The phrase “material disagreement” may be used descriptively for disagreement with a proposition, but does not itself determine guilt, canon-law penalties, or full communion; “formal dissent” likewise should not be used as a shortcut to pronounce a particular person's canonical status.

### E. Remaining Catholic while disagreeing

The user wondered whether continuing to belong to Catholic community remains possible while objecting to aspects of sexual complementarity, contraception ethics/NFP, gender discourse, and ecclesiastical moral authority. She has retained meaningful Catholic relationships and community roles while questioning parts of the theological framework.

**The careful answer:** baptism is not erased by intellectual struggle. Catholic communion, however, also includes bonds of profession of faith, sacraments, and governance (canons 204–205). Sincere doubt is not automatically canonical heresy or excommunication; neither does identifying as Catholic turn all contrary claims into permissible Church teaching. Sacramental discipline and assessment of a particular person's canonical circumstances require facts and pastoral/canonical judgment not supplied by this discussion. The question “Can I continue seeking a home here?” is not identical to “Does the institution doctrinally endorse every conclusion I have reached?”

### F. Pastoral participation by transgender people is not a one-word verdict on gender theology

The 2023 DDF responses explicitly permit baptism for a transgender person under stated conditions and provide qualified guidance regarding godparent service and marriage witnesses. Those concrete pastoral answers show that **participation, doctrinal approval, and sacramental eligibility must not be collapsed into one yes/no category**. Similarly, the 2024 *Dignitas infinita* §55 insists on personal dignity and rejection of unjust discrimination even as §§56–60 critique gender theory and sex-change interventions. The documents contain **both** commitments. Selecting only one side misrepresents the source.

## 6. Argument map: what follows, what does not

|:--|:--|:--|

| “A Catholic must act as though sincerely convinced even when not convinced.” | **Not supported as an account of free interior faith.** Catholic sources reject coerced belief; they still prescribe obligations of assent and formation. | External compliance, sincere assent, and unresolved conscience are distinguishable; one should not falsely claim an interior conviction. |
| “Conscience always makes my decision objectively right.” | **Not supported.** Conscience binds action but may be mistaken and needs formation. | Follow certain conscience responsibly while pursuing truth and taking authoritative teaching seriously. |
| “Respectful questions or qualified criticism prove heresy.” | **Not supported.** Canon 751 concerns obstinate denial/doubt of truths owed divine and Catholic faith; the faithful also have qualified expressive rights. | Identify the precise proposition, its theological note, and the actual manner of disagreement before attaching a label. |
| “The Church does not teach anything significant about biological sex.” | **Not supported.** The *Catechism* and *Dignitas infinita* plainly teach embodied sex difference. | Acknowledge the official anthropology without claiming it answers every dispute about language or experience. |
| “A female person who sometimes uses she/they necessarily repudiates her sex or marriage.” | **Not established in the reviewed magisterial sources.** | Ask what the pronouns mean to that actual person; do not infer a complete metaphysics or deceptive intent from the pronouns alone. |
| “Every gender stereotype is binding because Catholic teaching values complementarity.” | **Not supported.** Not all social customs or extrapolations are doctrinal propositions. | Distinguish Church teaching on sexed embodiment from contingent expectations about personality, dress, work, or household role. |

## 7. A practical process for resolving an authority dispute

1. **Quote the exact claim.** Is the disagreement about an actual sentence in an official text or someone's interpretation of it?
2. **Identify the speaker and genre.** Council, pope, dicastery, bishop, canon-law rule, theological scholar, or private individual?
3. **Specify the proposition's doctrinal level.** Is it proposed as revealed, definitively held, authentic non-definitive doctrine, discipline, contingent application, or non-magisterial argument? Avoid assigning level solely by document title.
4. **Distinguish the moral principle from an inference.** “Sex is meaningful” is not itself proof of “this pronoun use is a denial of sex”; “NFP is permitted” is not proof “our way of practicing it is equitable.”
5. **State precisely what the person believes.** Do not substitute presumed metaphysics for a first-person account of meaning or intent.
6. **Mark the kind of difficulty.** Unanswered question, competing interpretation, inability to give assent, contrary moral conclusion, proposed public teaching, or disciplinary conflict.
7. **Treat conscience seriously but not as omniscience.** Formation, reflection, evidence, freedom from coercion, and openness to correction all matter.
8. **Use appropriate channels for disagreement.** Competent scholarship, respectful pastoral dialogue, and the qualified speech recognized by canon 212 are not the same as claiming a rival magisterial authority.
9. **Do not make the relationship into a doctrinal tribunal.** One spouse may have a serious theological concern without having authority to pronounce the other's interior fidelity, motives, or worth.

## 8. Remaining unsettled questions (not falsely “resolved”)

- What exactly does **she/they** signify for this person, and what does it *not* signify? The magisterial texts reviewed do not provide a one-to-one rule for that choice.
- Which account of **complementarity** is disputed: bodily difference, mutuality, gender role stereotypes, reproductive function, or moral obligations? Different answers imply different doctrinal tensions.
- On **NFP**, is the objection about the intrinsic moral claim distinguishing contraception from periodic continence, the practice's actual harms, or both? These are related but separable questions.
- What does genuine **religious submission** look like when an individual has conscientiously studied a non-definitive teaching and still cannot give interior assent? *Donum veritatis* acknowledges this tension but does not make disagreement entirely costless or optional.
- What forms of **Catholic belonging, ministry, and sacramental practice** remain possible in an individual's concrete circumstances? This needs precise personal and pastoral assessment rather than an automatic answer from a chart.
- How should a couple discuss real **theological difference without converting it into accusations about betrayal, secrecy, or personhood**? The documents on authority do not license mind-reading.

## 9. Concise conclusions

1. **Three doctrinal levels of assent are official; not every Catholic text or clerical inference belongs at the same level.**
2. **Non-definitive is not optional; respectful difficulty is not identical to open-ended permission for dissent.**
3. **Conscience is binding in action and must be formed; coerced declarations do not create authentic belief.**
4. **Catholic sources articulate a meaningful embodied sexual anthropology; they do not, in the sources examined, explicitly classify occasional she/they use by a biologically female person as rejecting that anthropology.**
5. **The user's concern about complementarity as a normative ideal and about contraception raises real possible conflict with Catholic moral teaching; it should not be rhetorically erased.**
6. **Her lived experience of harms from NFP, unequal reproductive burdens, and the right not to be assigned a belief or intention she has not endorsed deserve separate moral attention.**
7. **Remaining connected to Catholic faith and community while wrestling with doctrine is a different question from claiming official authorization for every personal conviction.**
