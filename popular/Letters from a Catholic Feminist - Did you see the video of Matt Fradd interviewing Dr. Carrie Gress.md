# Did you see the video of Matt Fradd interviewing Dr. Carrie Gress?

Sure did. Here are my thoughts.

![Image from source PDF, page 1](assets/letters-from-a-catholic-feminist-did-you-see-the-video-of-matt-fradd-interviewing-dr-carrie-gress/page-01-X12.png)

CLAIRE THE CATHOLIC FEMINIST

DEC 13, 2023 ∙ PAID

![Image from source PDF, page 1](assets/letters-from-a-catholic-feminist-did-you-see-the-video-of-matt-fradd-interviewing-dr-carrie-gress/page-01-X14.jpg)

Quick note: when I first sent this, I embarrassingly had Matt’s name as “Matt Fraud” in the headline. This was truly a typo and not some catty snark-remark. I am legit so embarrassed—mea culpa.

Many people have sent me the recent interview Matt Fradd did with anti-feminism author Carrie Gress. I donʼt usually watch a ton of Catholic YouTube (sourdough YouTube, on the other hand…) but I did find the prospect of the conversation intriguing enough to watch it and see if I had thoughts.

<!-- PDF page 2 -->

And oh, the thoughts. The thoughts, my friends.

I canʼt overstate how important I think it is for people of goodwill to have conversations about the place of women in the church, and I feel like this interview highlights why Catholic feminism is important more than anything Iʼve seen lately.

## Before I proceed, I want to be clear about a few things up front.

I truly believe both Carrie Gress and Matt Fradd are operating in good faith. I canʼt know this, of course; Iʼm just going off gut feeling and the totality of their work. Thatʼs why Iʼm even taking the time to watch this and respond to it (that and because Iʼm a nosey Nellie). I think theyʼre both coming from a place of genuinely trying to live out a Christian life and help others do the same; you will never, ever find me responding to a Matt Walsh video. Itʼs my belief that both Carrie and Matt want women to be safe and cared for.

I have briefly met Matt at two conferences. He is a much bigger deal than me and surely doesnʼt remember this, ha. He was friendly and polite. His wife Cameron came on the Catholic Feminist pilgrimage to France and was absolutely lovely. I still use a parenting tip she gave me about teaching your kids to forgive one another, and I had a ton of fun with her.

Iʼve never read Carrieʼs books. If theyʼre on audio I might try to tackle that this year; I have many long commutes ahead of me in the coming days. I honestly have no desire to after listening to this interview, but Iʼd take one for the team if Letters subscribers were interested in my thoughts.

Matt has said some things in the past about women before that I find really distasteful, and I donʼt find the way he talks about the LGBTQ community charitable or inviting.

<!-- PDF page 3 -->

Mattʼs anti-porn work, which I think is his best stuff, has deeply helped multiple men I personally know and I think heʼs a revolutionary in that field. I donʼt know of a single person with the breadth and depth of knowledge he has on that topic, who also emphasize compassion and care for the men affected, and Iʼm really grateful his voice on it exists in the Catholic world.

OK? Sound good?

![Image from source PDF, page 3](assets/letters-from-a-catholic-feminist-did-you-see-the-video-of-matt-fradd-interviewing-dr-carrie-gress/page-03-X24.jpg)

But before my take, hereʼs a not-at-all-short detour in the form of a TLDR summary of Carrie and Mattʼs main points. This is not an extensive, total

### covering of everything they discussed and I apologize if I missed something vital.

Carrie describes how she went into researching first-wave feminism believing sheʼd find a form of “good feminismˮ that went off the rails, but actually found that feminism has been rotten from the beginning.

Matt says anyone who calls themselves a “Catholic feministˮ is like saying theyʼre a “Catholic Marxistˮ, and that you actually canʼt be a Catholic feminist. He directly says both of these things: “people can use terms however they wish to use themˮ and “Catholic feminism is an oxymoronˮ.

Carrie says that first-wave feminism wasnʼt about helping women as women, it was about how to make women more like men. She says weʼve made masculinity an idol.

<!-- PDF page 4 -->

Carrie names Mary Wollstonecraft as the “first feministˮ even though the word didnʼt exist in her time. She then dives into another early feminist, Mary Shelley, who was interested in the occult. Carrie claims that Mary Shelley and and Mary Wollstonecraft are sort of the founding fathers of feminism. She claims that their love of the occult became the “modelˮ for future waves of feminism (again, noting that feminism as a word did not exist at this time). Matt says that modern feminists wonʼt be turned off by this at all, we all would think it was good as we like Satan.

Carrie says that patriarchy is how we bring “salvation out of fallennessˮ, and that it shouldnʼt be considered a bad thing. Itʼs just that “men bring order to society and the family.ˮ

Matt asks if women truly need to be liberated from oppression and Carrie Gress laughs and says that men have actually suffered terribly from women. She says that people have simply “wandered from the Ten Commandmentsˮ and that what we need to do is invite men to be men and women to be women.

They dive back into history, critiquing Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Susan B. Anthony quite extensively. She talks about how Stanton also played around with the occult, utilizing “spirit tablesˮ to communicate with supernatural beings.

<!-- PDF page 5 -->

Carrie draws lines between the blending of communism and feminism in the second wave of feminism, and Matt points out that in America today, since there are abortions on demand and “women go to work and someone else looks after their kidsˮ, weʼre living out a form of Marxism. Matt also points out that women frequently ask men to “bend over apologizing profusely to no prevailˮ. Carrie goes through most of the second-wave feminists whose names you know Betty Friedan, Simone de Beauvoir, Kate Millett). They emphasize second-wave feminismʼs desire to make women more masculine. Matt points out that Simone de Beauvoir thought women shouldnʼt have a choice to stay home with their kids and basically believed that the state should raise children.

Carrie insinuates that women were probably unhappy in their marriages because they chose to go to college instead of learning how to raise children. They talk about the second-wave feminists for quite a while, almost a third of the video. Definitely go watch the interview if you want more depth on de Beauvoir and Millett. Dr. Gress clearly did a ton of research and knows her history).

Matt claims that feminism is its own religion and Carrie counters, calling it a “cultˮ and claiming that nobody is allowed to “questionˮ feminism, while on a very popular YouTube show questioning feminism.

Matt says that what porn has done for men is what feminism has done for women. He says that Carrieʼs book is an act of compassion, the same way we need to have compassion for men who view porn. Carrie says women arenʼt making active choices to embrace feminism, it was just “shoved down our throatsˮ. Carrie says that she really cares for women living the hook-up culture who are miserable and depressed, and that she believes we need to offer a better way out of that for women.

<!-- PDF page 6 -->

Carrie says feminism has tried to erase motherhood, and that we need to reclaim it—even women who arenʼt mothers are called to spiritual motherhood, mentoring, etc. She says itʼs un-objectively better for women to be home with their kids yet also says that God is asking her to be realistic and live in the world sheʼs living in, and that sheʼd prefer to be a stay-at-home mom but is here talking to Matt. She says it doesnʼt have to be “stay-at-homeˮ vs. “workˮ as a binary choice.

They talk about the idea of wives submitting to their husbands and what that should mean vs. what Paul was saying vs. what we hear about at conferences.

Carrie makes the argument that feminism is what gave birth to gender ideology: since women are trying to become like men, theyʼre detaching themselves from their feminine attributes and once thatʼs all torn out, whatʼs left? “This is why we donʼt know what a woman is.ˮ

Carrie says that feminism has “strict confinesˮ. She says that we shouldnʼt say weʼre feminists, we should just say weʼre Catholic. She says that “feminine geniusˮ is overused. Matt says when women say they “want to be understoodˮ they need to “stop whiningˮ.

Carrie says that Mary is the antidote to feminism. She says that we often think of her as unrelatable or removed, but that she was a real women who is very connected to our lives.

Matt brings it back to why we need to work towards women not working outside the home, and says that the only reason women “needˮ to work outside the home now is because of the consequences of sin.

<!-- PDF page 7 -->

Carrie says that families should vote as families and that women voting “promotes individualismˮ. Matt says the head of the household should vote on behalf of the family. Carrie says that she doesnʼt have a firm answer on this and hasnʼt done a lot of research on it.

In sum, she describes the history of feminism as one of scrappy groups of sinning women fighting against oppressors in ways that at times went way, way off the rails. She believes that the world is deeply hurting and that feminism has played a role in that hurt.

Yes, and.

Carrieʼs take on feminism is not unpopular or unique. She continuously throughout the interview—like, very, very frequently—points out how this stuff is “never discussedˮ and “no one talks about itˮ, how nobody knew the information sheʼs currently presenting. This is untrue. Catholics have been discussing feminism for many years, and this was not the first time I heard a single point she raised. She embraces the culture-warrior habit of gesturing towards a narrative, as if public discourse isnʼt thousands of warring narratives but really just one Big Bad Narrative we all need to push against.

## I think Carrie makes a handful of valuable points, and she correctly identifies some problems within society and the feminism movement.

The history of feminism involves plenty of unsavory characters. The early feminists were far from saints; Susan B. Anthony made plenty of racist remarks while working for universal suffrage and Elizabeth Cady Stanton was indeed kinda witchy.

And yet—so does the United States of America. But if someone were to bring up how Thomas Jefferson owned people, I feel strongly that Matt and Carrieʼs responses would be that people can bring about both good and bad fruit, and that we were being woke, anachronistic whiners! This

<!-- PDF page 8 -->

standard of perfection is used by both sides of the political aisle in a very obnoxious way. Either we believe people can be good and bad, can contribute worthwhile things to the world and be sinners, or we donʼt. Carrieʼs belief that the errors of first-wave (and pre-first-wave) feminists leads to a rotten movement doesnʼt hold water. Feminism has absolutely had mistaken leaders throughout its history. Franciscan University, where Carrie Gress went and Matt Fradd frequently speaks, has had plenty as well. This is not a case of what-aboutism; itʼs simply pointing out the inconsistency of her logic.

Carrie does, however, correctly identify many problems within the second-wave feminism movement. She also states many times that people who donʼt want to be feminists arenʼt horrible people, and I agree with her wholeheartedly. People need to calm down about the labels they assign or don’t assign to themselves. Who cares if someone hates the word “feministˮ? Iʼm much more disturbed that Carrie and Matt, two people who can vote, are seemingly against womenʼs suffrage than the fact that they don't want to identify as feminists.

The best part of the interview is when they dive deep into the reality of abortion. When you hear the staggering statistics about just how many people are being killed every single year, itʼs hard to deny that many secular feminists, who see masculinity as the ideal, have contributed significantly to that. Carrie and Matt both care deeply about this, and also seem to see post-abortive women who were fed lies about motherhood in their proper role as victims of a system.

Lastly, Carrie clearly has an immense love for our mother that I find inspiring. Iʼve been working on cultivating my relationship with Mary over the past few years and I think looking to Mary as an antidote for the worldʼs problems is something all Catholics should strive to do.

## But I came away from this interview under the impression that Carrie and Matt missed

<!-- PDF page 9 -->

## something fundamental within their conversation.

There were three main issues I had with Carrie and Mattʼs chat that seemed to permeate the entire length of the two hour interview.

## First of all, the truth about womenʼs oppression was never addressed.

Perhaps I missed it. But I donʼt believe we ever received an answer to what the hell we’re supposed to do, then.

In fact, oftentimes when Matt brought up womenʼs oppression, he would say it in this semi-sarcastic tone and Carrie would kind of smirk. The clear implication was that women arenʼt really oppressed, and that weʼre basically, in Mattʼs direct words, “whiningˮ. Iʼm truly wondering if theyʼve ever sat down and done real research about the pain and anguish women around the world are facing in a way that doesnʼt already comply with their worldview. The pain they seem willing to acknowledge is the pain of women who felt pressured by culture to have an abortion, or the pain of new mothers when adjusting to the demands of family life. They also briefly acknowledge women who live in truly totalitarian systems, like Afghans or Iranians.

But the everyday pain of women never gets addressed. Matt says that maybe women might suffer more domestic abuse than men, which is almost laughable¹. 1 in 3 women worldwide are victims of male violence. In the US alone, nearly 20 people a minute are physically abused by an intimate partner—and the vast majority of these are women. And thatʼs not even mentioning healthcare discrimination, sexual exploitation, and the myriad of other ways women around the country and world are suffering. If Carrie does not feel oppressed, thatʼs wonderful. Truly—I am so glad, because sheʼs a daughter of God and thatʼs what she deserves. But our faith doesnʼt allow us to just kick back and not care for the oppressed just because we (and those in our immediate circle) arenʼt

<!-- PDF page 10 -->

suffering from oppression! It gives off the vibes of a bunch of rich guys sitting at a banquet going, “Well, there arenʼt really poor people, are there?ˮ while Lazarus bleeds to death on the front stoop.

Matt makes the bold claim early on in the video that feminism is needed because women are weaker—but he contradicts himself when he goes onto claim that women donʼt actually need protection! His emphasis on how women are more vulnerable, which isnʼt a deficiency, was one of the best things he said in the interview. But then why would he later claim that theyʼre only “maybeˮ victims of domestic violence, and that they donʼt need any organized effort to protect them? I know Matt believes in the fatherhood crisis, because Iʼve heard him call out men many, many times on his show and in talks. So then why, if men are so obviously failing to step up to their roles as husbands and fathers, are we refusing to acknowledge that women might be in need of protection through a movement like feminism!? Yes, women are more vulnerable and phsyically “weakerˮ! Thatʼs why theyʼre so often the victims of male-inflicted violence, a fact that he and Carrie glossed over at best!

## Secondly, women in the workplace utilizing the “feminine geniusˮ may not be your cuppa tea, but they arenʼt anti- Catholic or unideal.

I truly canʼt wait for the day all of the people who are so passionately against women in the workplace meet St. Gianna or St. Zelie in Heaven. The cognitive dissonance required for a woman who has written multiple books, gives many talks, and traveled away from her family to be on a famous podcast to sit and say that we should work towards a world where women donʼt work outside the home is honestly baffling.

And if you do believe in real differences between men and women, which I believe Matt and Carrie do, why are you opposed to Saint John Paul the Great coining the gifts that women bring forth the “feminine geniusˮ? Why is it “patronizingˮ, in Carrieʼs words, to believe women

<!-- PDF page 11 -->

bring forth unique gifts if there really are true differences between men and women? Are we different or arenʼt we?

## Lastly, I believe the views of someone like Carrie Gress are doing just as much to promote modern gender ideology as the views of the “wokeˮ who she so despises.

Modern-day, secular feminists give a superficial answer to the often-proposed question, “What is a woman?ˮ The answer seems to be “whatever you want!ˮ and the solution proposed is that we can all choose our gender.

But Carrieʼs answer isnʼt any deeper. Her reliance on stereotypical gender roles—a woman in the kitchen, a man at work—is also a watered down answer to the question. She goes so far as to dismiss the “feminine geniusˮ, in fact, which seems to imply that what women really have to offer isnʼt all that special or complicated.

Let me provide an example. I know a young girl (this is a true story, but Iʼm going to be as vague as possible) who is very athletic. She prefers to hang out with boys. She never wears dresses. Her favorite game to play at recess is knights and dragons. Sheʼs quite loud, confrontational, and hilarious. She wants to be a doctor or a soccer player when she grows up. Both the modern gender ideologists and Carrie Gress would claim that she is “less womanˮ than someone like me, who loves to rock a sundress, bakes homemade treats for her children almost every day, and spends half the week as a stay-at-home mom. They place the beauty of her womanhood in a list of practical traits to check off instead of a deep, inner gift from God that can look a variety of ways.

But that girl is a girl. She has the feminine genius. She is called to motherhood, whether itʼs biological or not.

It isnʼt just modern culture telling this little girl sheʼs not really a girl—itʼs people like Carrie.

The world is a mess. Weʼve in many ways abandoned the design God had for men and women, weʼve hung our hats on the vital importance of women being able to end the lives of their unborn children, and mental illnesses like depression have absolutely skyrocketed in the muck + mud of the trauma weʼre inflicting on one another.

Yes, and. There are holy and unholy ways of solving these problems. Fourth wave feminism could involve women who genuinely care for one another, love men, and fight to “bring good news to the poor, heal the brokenhearted, and proclaim liberty to captivesˮ. But it can only be that way if we keep our eyes on Jesus, and our hearts open to the women of the world.

I call that feminism.
