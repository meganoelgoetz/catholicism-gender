# Carrie Gress Is Still Wrong

Guess who's back-back-back, back again-gain-gain

CLAIRE THE CATHOLIC FEMINIST

JAN 30, 2025 ∙ PAID

![Image from source PDF, page 1](assets/letters-from-a-catholic-feminist-carrie-gress-is-still-wrong/page-01-X14.jpg)

## I have zero desire to turn this newsletter into a Matt Fradd Reaction Cyclone (although wow, you all love those) and I know you donʼt want that either.

But Carrieʼs back on Matt Fradd and thatʼs how I spent my morning.

Look, I took it to prayer and do think that I have something to offer in this sphere. I donʼt ever aim to tell you what to think but instead I attempt to demonstrate a way of thinking: a process for engaging with ideas that allows you plenty of room for disagreement without diving into that sweet, sweet cesspool of sin and middle fingers. If nothing else, Iʼm starting to feel like my mission with this newsletter is just to show people that every single human is just a flailing ball of emotions trying their

<!-- PDF page 2 -->

best, but the more and more we can dig deeper than those emotions to our true identity as sons and daughters of Christ, the more fulfilled weʼll be, and the closer weʼll be to what we were meant to be.

Secondly, they spent lot of time discussing “publications with Catholic and feminist in the nameˮ and while Iʼm obviously not the only platform, Iʼm one of the larger. So, yʼknow. Invite me into the ring and Iʼll climb right in¹.

Iʼm not going to do the throat-clearing I did last time; all of the disclaimers that I donʼt read hearts and I donʼt think Matt Fradd is a total grifter. I think he has done some things that are very helpful to the church. His book The Porn Myth is brilliant. Iʼve met him twice (very briefly, years ago, and he almost certainly does not remember) and he was very gracious. I have never read Carrieʼs books, and can only speak into her public interviews.

What follows are my opinions on an interview between Matt Fradd, an incredibly popular Catholic YouTuber, and Carrie Gress, a working mother with a PHD who travels for her career while promoting the idea that women should stay home with their children full-time.

I also apologize that thereʼs no way on earth I could completely recap a 2 hour + interview. I highlighted what I see as the highlights but there are many other things that could be discussed. Iʼm sorry if I missed something you found vital—let me know in the comments.

Lastly, to be brutally honest, this interview was less interesting than their first and essentially a rehashing of their first conversation with a lot of side anecdotes. It kind of meandered on things that I wasnʼt sure had to do with the bigger picture—that men and not women mainly go into construction work, and that people with dyed hair are fighting against nature, and that Matt saw some prostitutes in Switzerland . . . if I squint I can kind of see the threads of arguments they were trying to make but

<!-- PDF page 3 -->

man, I think Mattʼs conversations would benefit from some serious focus and editing². It also spent a lot of time talking about her critics, and what bothers me is that I do feel as if I and others have bent over backwards trying to be generous to a woman we see as getting a lot wrong, and not feeling like I got that charity back. But—hey. I run a newsletter called the Catholic Feminist. The skin is #thick.

We begin:

## Carrie Gress has decided that her definition of feminism is the correct one, while simultaneously insisting that itʼs a shape-shifting term.

A theme youʼll see here is that there were many times throughout Carrieʼs interview that I found her self-contradictory, and this is the main one. Carrie both says that her extremely narrow definition of feminism is the only one and that people just say feminism means whatever they wants. She says multiple times that feminism is the belief that women should separate men and children, because life would be better without men. This definition is accepted by almost nobody who calls themselves a feminist, and it seems to me that you should at least ask the people claiming the title what they believe feminism is. Otherwise, youʼre arguing with a straw man. Even the most liberal of feminists, many of whom I have far more serious disagreements with than I do with Carrie, would not identify with that definition. Itʼs a definition made for the applause of those who want to dunk on it, which I find distasteful and, to be frank, an excuse to avoid getting your fingernails dirty with the other sideʼs argument.

Thereʼs simply not evidence of a mass movement of women insisting on their dominance over men. Are there some women who think thatʼs the ideal? Okay. Sure. But itʼs ironic to me that someone who has built a career (and reminder: other women, according to Carrie, should not have as robust a career) on insisting the patriarchy is so beneficial doesnʼt see the lack of logic in saying that a few women in a movement

<!-- PDF page 4 -->

say women should dominate, ergo all feminists believe that, while a few men in a movement say men should beat the shit out of women, but of course thatʼs just the Manosphere weirdos, not the patriarchy at large! Which one is it? As Trent Horn has said, you should always be comparing best to best and worst to worst, not best to worst within a movement.

## Carrie continues to insist that first wave feminism had absolutely no benefits, and was only for the worse.

I am not exaggerating to make a point; she literally says many times that first wave feminism was evil. In this particular interview, she points out that many of the supporters of the suffrage movement were also abolitionists. She says that because of this, they couldnʼt see the difference between the practice of owning a person vs. the family unit, but the obvious response to this is that men basically did own their wives! Women couldnʼt own property, couldnʼt vote for their government, couldnʼt leave abusers, couldnʼt have custody of their children, couldnʼt fight back if they were involuntarily put in mental homes for crazy things like reading, etc. Iʼm confused if Carrie thinks those things are okay, and if she doesnʼt, what she would have suggested to do instead of the first wave feminism movement. She says that things like the hunger strikes and burning of buildings were “vileˮ³, while never conceding that women were completely powerless in civil society to make any changes to laws. Sure, you can claim they influenced culture through influencing their family, but Iʼd like to remind you that the culture at large believed women were basically ornamental trinkets men kept around to make heirs. They were not these respected, honored members of the family in many, many cases. Pre-first-wave-feminism was not some blissful utopia of families. Please, for the love of God⁴, engage with this history.

Carrie said that many of her Catholic critics are defending first wave feminism without really knowing what it is, an argument I frankly find insulting. Erika Bachiochi and Abigail Favale know what theyʼre talking

<!-- PDF page 5 -->

about. Erika wrote an entire book on Mary Wollstonecraft, and it feels quite lazy to say that your critics just “donʼt understandˮ the full picture. Saying that feminism canʼt lead to care because itʼs all about ending the family would mean that “ending the familyˮ is what most feminists believe, which it, again, is not.

Of course many of the first wave feminists had flaws. Of course they said somethings that werenʼt aligned with the church. But as I said in my last letter regarding Carrie, she (and Matt) have a different standard for feminism than they have for any other project. The founders of America owned human beings and yet I strongly doubt either Carrie or Matt would point to that as why America as a political project shouldnʼt exist. Carrie has obviously done vast amounts of research and loves to go into detailed stories about why this suffrage or that had a hard, miserable life, without ever acknowledging that a) the patriarchal systems they were fighting against played into that misery and trauma and b) the flaws of movement heads do not necessarily disparage the movement itself. Itʼs anecdotal evidence, cherry-picking stories, and blatantly ignoring the struggles and indignity of the lives these women led.

“Thatʼs why you need pepper spray, darling,ˮ Matt smirks as they discuss that a woman could never kick a manʼs ass. Okay. So, what were the beaten women, back when it was legally allowed for men to smack their wives around, supposed to do?

She insists throughout the interview that we would have made these equality advances anyway due to technology, but provides absolutely no evidence of that. She also states that she doesnʼt have feminism to thank for her PHD because things were “heading in that directionˮ without feminism—again, providing absolutely no data or meat to back that up. Itʼs an interesting hypothesis but thatʼs all it is.

## I had the hardest time staying regulated when Carrie went off on Saint John Paul the Great.

<!-- PDF page 6 -->

Iʼm not going to lie, I might have shrieked at the computer when Carrie said that John Paul the Second “maybe didnʼt mean itˮ in the “one placeˮ he talked about feminism.

First of all, she got the place where he discussed the phrase new feminism wrong—itʼs in Evangelium Vitae, not Veritatis Splendor. Which, fine, everyone makes mistakes, but that lets me know sheʼs not super familiar with JP2ʼs writings on women. If youʼre making a career out of arguing against feminism to Catholics, you should probably be a bit more prepared to discuss JP2, who Catholic feminists have basically adopted as our patron.

Secondly, JP2 might have only used that phrase in one place, but he talked about women extensively, in many different documents and sermons. Her argument is that if he didnʼt use the phrase feminism in Mulieris Dignitatum, itʼs not “his thingˮ. When Matt Fradd asked her about the context of the phrase new feminism, she said she didnʼt know the “exact contextˮ and danced around it.

Well, Iʼll let you know the exact context—John Paul the Secondʼs full quote is this:

### “In transforming culture so that it supports life, women occupy a place, in thought and action, which is unique and decisive. It depends on them to promote a "new feminism" which rejects the temptation of imitating models of "male domination", in order to acknowledge and affirm the true genius of women in every aspect of the life of society, and overcome all discrimination, violence and exploitation.ˮ

You can see why Carrie wouldnʼt want to discuss that. She does not believe women should reject the model of “male dominationˮ or participate in “every aspect of the life of societyˮ—at least, she doesnʼt come across as believing those things. You donʼt have to agree with everything a pope says or does. I have fundamental disagreements with

<!-- PDF page 7 -->

plenty of popes. But it comes across as so disingenuous the way she shrugs off the question! It was certainly the part of the interview where I got the most frustrated. Defend your arguments.

## There seems to be some cognitive dissonance with Carrie and Mattʼs professed beliefs and how they live them out.

We all have some cognitive dissonance. But it seemed jarring in this interview. It was like theyʼd say something kind of outlandish, then feel the need to cover their bases and quickly say something kind of opposite, then double down on what they originally said. Maybe theyʼre just walking through ideas and thinking out loud but it made for an odd conversation.

Matt and Carrie continue to grip onto stereotypical definitions of manhood and womanhood in a manner I find exhausting. Yes, all women are called to serve and keep the home—but there is absolutely no biblical or church standard that claims that involves women cooking diner every night, which Matt brings up when he says women need to “figure it outˮ if theyʼre uncomfortable with what he perceives to be their roles. And Carrie saying that we judge bad dads but not bad moms had my jaw on the floor—I think few people would disagree that our culture is constantly judging parents of both genders.

But: I also think how Carrie said women like to be drawn along by culture is true. Women do not respond well to being yelled at from a stage— weʼre much more influenced by movies, magazines, influencers, etc. that kind of set the tone for the culture. I just thought that was interesting to think about. Thereʼs a really deep need right now for art and culture that reflect Catholic truths, and Iʼm excited to see how many young artists are taking part in that. I actually think her brand, Theology of Home, has a cool aesthetic. She compliments other women-led organizations, like Walking With Purpose and Catholic Women in Business—but I donʼt understand how she reconciles that with her belief that women shouldnʼt work outside the home!

<!-- PDF page 8 -->

And later on, Matt does say that we shouldnʼt shame men who like poetry, or women like hunting—that we shouldnʼt judge marriages based on these types of stereotypes. “We say men can only be like this, and women should only be like this…weʼve got to be really careful with that.ˮ And it actually really touched my heart when he talked about the importance of physical love towards children, even though they age. The guy is clearly wild about his family. Itʼs just confusing to me because I feel like he stereotypes consistently, but I do have to state that near the end of the interview he emphasizes that point. It leaves me wondering if the confidence with which they make their statements doesnʼt reflect the actual positioning of their hearts. I could be off-base here; Iʼm just giving my reaction.

## To conclude—what is feminism bringing to Catholicism?

Thatʼs what Carrie asks.

Here is my answer: what is being pro-life bringing to Catholicism? What is being anti-death-penalty bringing to Catholicism? What is being anti-discrimination bringing to Catholicism? Nothing! These things arenʼt bringing things to the faith, theyʼre aspects of Godʼs truth that are often ignored. As Carrie correctly points out, Catholic women abort at similar percentages to non-Catholic women. This is a tragedy that has certainly been supported and celebrated by secular feminism. To be pro-life is to vocally stand against the death of the unborn—itʼs a facet of your Catholicism, not a qualifier of it. Iʼm a feminist because Iʼm a Catholic, Iʼm not a Catholic Feminist because I think my catholicism is lacking. Itʼs not a “watering downˮ of the faith, itʼs the fullest version of it.

## Carrie and I identify so many of the same problems, but she insists that the answer is simplistic and I do not.

Were Carrie Gress and I to sit down with a glass of wine, we would agree on so much. I really believe that. I think we both love Mary. I think we both want to end violence against women. I think we both believe abortion is bad for women. I think we both believe in modesty. I think we

<!-- PDF page 9 -->

both believe fashion magazines are influencing women in a negative way. I think we both believe femininity involves the beautiful gift of service. I think we both believe prostitution is bad for women. I think we both believe contraception is bad for women. I think we both believe the manosphere is toxic I was thankful that was brought up this go-around.)

I think itʼs great that Carrie is pointing out flaws that secular feminism has brought about, and we can always use more of that. I want to link arms with anyone that cares about families and wants to have conversations about how to better support the family unit, especially with people who have such a devotion to the blessed mother.

But Carrie believes that all of this “freedomˮ women have fought for has led to all of this—the despair among women, the lack of health among women, the general uneasiness so many women feel. She cannot name a single thing that feminism has gotten right when asked. Itʼs the be all end all evil; the solution to all of earthʼs difficulties.

But as good ole JP2 said, “freedom consists not in doing what we like, but in having the right to do what we ought.ˮ⁵ These women who are aborting, abandoning their families, or living in selfishness are not free. Theyʼre slaves—not slaves to feminism, but slaves to sin. Theyʼre not understanding their true identity and purpose as a beloved daughter. Catholic feminists are fighting for true freedom: a freedom in knowing you whose you are. In knowing that you are a person made in the image of Christ capable of virtue, self-sacrifice, and all of the good and splendid and painful things a life with Christ has to offer. Carrie says that feminism is about power, while the church is about surrender—but doesnʼt mention what weʼre surrendering to. Not our bodily weaknesses; not our natural tendencies towards sin; not oppressive structures or abusive husbands—we are surrendering to JESUS. That is Catholic feminism.

<!-- PDF page 10 -->

Eliminating workplace discrimination, securing the right to vote, allowing women to own property—these things were never going to eliminate sin. They werenʼt meant to! They were meant to give women a choice, to live in their freedom or to choose a path of darkness. Women are rational, capable beings who are able to make those choices, to their benefit or peril. Of the many insulting things Iʼve heard fly out of Carrie Gressʼ mouth, this has to be the part I disagree with her most on: that women are simply victims of these circumstances, and that they are completely powerless to root out the good of feminism from the bad. If theyʼre allowed to vote, they will obviously vote “wrongˮ. If theyʼre allowed to own property, the family will obviously be destroyed. She doesnʼt say these things explicitly but if youʼre going to argue against first wave feminism, do it with your whole chest, please. These are the obvious conclusions. She claims that women are fighting to be like men I mean, yes, if men are able to choose their political leadership, leave abusive situations, and not be discriminated against in the workplace, I suppose you could say that those are some ways we should try to be “like menˮ. But in my opinion, thatʼs not trying to be like men, thatʼs exercising our basic human dignity.

## Iʼm going to say something weird.

The only thing propelling me towards reading Carrie Gressʼ books are that Iʼd like to learn more about her background. A person who looks at a complex project like feminism and analyzes it with such black and white binaries is generally someone whoʼs been through something painful. I donʼt know if thatʼs true. But itʼs something that struck me after watching.

Carrie says that all of us young Catholic women “donʼt want anything to do with feminismˮ. As we advocate against workplace discrimination, support pregnant mothers, argue for support for families, and fight against sexual violence—I beg to differ.

Paris by the Book by Liam Callanan: I really enjoyed Callananʼs When in Rome so I was looking forward to diving into this story of a woman searching for her missing husband and running a Parisian bookshop, but the ending was so dissatisfying that it really killed the book for me.

There Will Always Be a New Anti-Feminist Influencer: A perfect read to accompany todayʼs letter. “The most telling irony is that

### these influencersʼ careers embody everything they claim to stand against. Theyʼre entrepreneurs building personal brands, seeking spotlight and status, monetizing controversy, and often prioritizing work over traditional domestic life. Theyʼve carved out lucrative niches in the attention economy while preaching against womenʼs professional ambitions. Their success depends on the very feminist gains they denounce—the freedom to speak publicly, to build businesses, to influence social discourse. Theyʼre selling rebellion against modern womanhood, but their ability to sell it at all is thoroughly, unmistakably modern.ˮ

Care Isnʼt Just Undervalued; Itʼs Barely on the Map: A very solid take on the boneheaded idea that working moms do everything stay-at-home moms do, but in less time.
